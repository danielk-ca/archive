He happens to have the same surname, and one forename. The other parts of the names are different. We are factually different people, and I do not know this individual.

Our lawyer says we need to get a clearance letter to satisfy the lender/title insurer and close on time. The creditor's law firm has asked for about $480 to produce the letter. Attempts by our lawyer to get them to lower their price went unanswered. The closing date is on August 26, 2026

We will have to pay the absurdly high $480 rather than risk the transaction. I want to persue the creditor's law firm and seek reimbursement after the fact on principle. Is that realistic and how would I go about doing that?


This is a genuinely frustrating situation — you're being forced to pay for an administrative consequence of someone else's debt, purely because of a naming coincidence. Let me give you an honest assessment.

Realistic Prospects: Modest, But Not Zero on Principle

The hard truth first: the legal basis for recovery is weak. The creditor's law firm didn't act wrongfully — they held a validly registered writ, you needed their confirmation, and they provided it. Courts and regulators are unlikely to see $480 for a law firm letter as a clear-cut wrong. You paid voluntarily (even if under duress of the closing), and they delivered something you needed.

That said, "realistic" and "worth pursuing on principle" aren't always the same thing, and there are a few avenues worth understanding.

Avenues Worth Considering

1. A Direct Letter to BMO Corporate (Most Underrated Option)

The law firm acts on BMO's instructions. A well-written complaint letter to BMO's Office of the President or their complaint escalation department — framing this as a consumer fairness issue — occasionally produces goodwill reimbursements. BMO has reputational incentives that the external law firm does not. This costs you nothing but time. The key is to be factual, firm, and not overly aggressive.

2. Law Society of Ontario (LSO) Complaint

This is primarily a principle avenue, not a money-recovery one. The LSO's Rules of Professional Conduct require that fees charged to clients be fair and reasonable — but you are a third party, not their client, which weakens this angle considerably. However, if the firm is routinely charging third parties inflated fees for name-clearance letters as a revenue stream, the LSO may have an interest. Don't expect a refund from this route, but it creates a record.

3. Ontario Small Claims Court

You could file a claim — the $35,000 limit is not a barrier here. The problem is the legal theory. Possible arguments:

  • Unjust enrichment: they benefited at your expense with no legal justification. This is the strongest angle, but courts will note they provided a real service.
  • Economic duress: you paid under compulsion (closing deadline). This doctrine exists in Ontario contract law, but it's typically applied to more serious coercive conduct, not a business charging a high price for a letter.

Filing costs roughly $102 in Ontario Small Claims. Realistically, you would likely lose, or the firm wouldn't even contest it and might settle to avoid the hassle — but don't count on that.

4. Write to the Debtor Directly (Long Shot)

If the actual debtor could theoretically be found, they caused the downstream harm through their financial situation. In practice, someone with a $500K writ against them is unlikely to voluntarily reimburse you $480, and pursuing it would cost far more.

What to Do Right Now (Pre-Closing)

Before you pay, it's worth getting a simple written receipt or invoice from the creditor's law firm that clearly describes the payment as being for a "name clearance letter / statutory declaration confirming non-identity with judgment debtor." This documentation helps if you pursue any of the above later.

Isn't there an avenue under the Solicitor's Act to apply for an assessment of the bill to determine if it was fair and reasonable in all the circumstances of a case (hours, complexity, skill, outcome, etc.)? If so, how would that work?


You've identified a more sophisticated avenue than anything in my previous response, and it deserves a careful breakdown. The short answer is: yes, the mechanism exists and you're right to flag it — but there's a significant threshold problem with your specific situation.

The Solicitors Act, R.S.O. 1990, c. S.15 — How It Works

The Act gives the Superior Court of Justice the power to refer a solicitor's bill to an Assessment Officer — a court-appointed officer who examines whether a bill is fair and reasonable in all the circumstances, applying factors very much like you described:

  • Time and labour involved
  • Complexity and difficulty
  • Skill required
  • Importance of the matter to the client
  • Results achieved
  • Whether the amount charged was proportionate

The Assessment Officer issues a Certificate of Assessment, and if the bill is reduced, the difference must be refunded. The process is conducted at the Superior Court, and there are established rules governing it under both the Act and the Rules of Civil Procedure (Rule 58).

Time limit: If the bill has been paid, Section 4(1) of the Act generally imposes a 12-month window from the date of payment to apply. Since you're paying around August 26, 2026, you have time.

The Central Problem: Your Standing

Here is where your situation hits a wall that the Act doesn't clearly resolve.

The Act's assessment mechanism is written around the solicitor-client relationship. The operative phrase in Section 3(1) refers to "the party chargeable by a solicitor's bill." The question — which is a genuinely unsettled one in your fact pattern — is whether you, as a non-client third party who was effectively compelled to pay the creditor's law firm's charge, qualify as "the party chargeable."

The arguments on each side:

In your favour:

  • You are literally the party who was charged and who paid the bill. The plain language of "party chargeable" could arguably capture you.
  • The spirit of the Act is consumer protection against excessive legal fees, and your situation is one of practical compulsion — you had no ability to negotiate or refuse.
  • There is some jurisprudence suggesting assessment rights can extend beyond the strict client relationship in certain circumstances (e.g., where third parties are ordered to pay a solicitor's costs).

Against you:

  • You are not the client of this firm. They owe you no professional duty. Their retainer is with BMO or the judgment creditor.
  • Courts have historically interpreted assessment rights narrowly around the solicitor-client relationship.
  • The letter was arguably a service rendered to you — not a legal bill rendered against you in the traditional sense.

This standing question is the pivotal issue your lawyer would need to research, and it's not one I can answer with confidence. It may even be novel on these exact facts.

The Most Constructive Path

Given all of the above, if you want to pursue this on principle, the most efficient sequence would be:

  1. After closing, ask your own lawyer to give you a brief opinion on whether you have standing under the Solicitors Act given you are a non-client. This is a focused, narrow question that shouldn't take long to answer.
  2. If standing looks arguable, file the application yourself in Small Claims or Superior Court without legal representation — the Assessment Officer process itself is relatively accessible to self-represented parties for straightforward billing disputes.
  3. Simultaneously, send a complaint letter to both the LSO (as a fee-gouging concern) and to BMO's Office of the President. These cost nothing and may resolve it without formal proceedings.
  4. Document everything now — get a proper invoice from the creditor's law firm before you pay, clearly stating what the $480 covers. This is essential for any future assessment.

You were right to think of this, and it's a sharper instinct than most people would have. Whether it's viable in your specific non-client posture is genuinely uncertain — but that uncertainty alone is worth a short conversation with your lawyer post-closing.

Your strategic instinct here is sound, and yes — filing does impose real costs on them that almost certainly exceed $480.

What It Actually Costs Them

A law firm is not a typical Small Claims defendant, but they're still not immune to the economics. To defend the claim they would need to:

  • Have a lawyer review the claim and prepare a Defence (filed within 20 days of service)
  • Attend a mandatory Settlement Conference — this is the key one
  • Potentially attend a trial if no settlement is reached

Even if they send a junior associate or articling student, the internal cost of a Settlement Conference alone — preparation plus attendance plus travel — easily runs 2-4 hours. At even a modest $200-250/hour internal rate, that's $400-$1,000 in firm resources to defend a $480 claim. The math works in your favour as a pressure tactic.

The Settlement Conference Is Your Real Leverage

Ontario Small Claims Court requires a mandatory Settlement Conference before any trial can proceed. A Deputy Judge presides and actively pushes both sides toward resolution. At that point, a rational law firm calculates: "We can settle for $240 and close this file, or spend another $800 in time preparing for and attending trial." That's where your leverage is highest.

Your Downside Risk Is Minimal

If you lose outright at trial, costs awards in Small Claims are capped and modest — realistically $72-120 on a $480 claim (15% representation fee plus minor disbursements). Your total worst-case exposure is roughly $200-250 all in. You're not betting the farm.

Do This Before Filing

Send a formal written demand letter to the firm first — after closing, with no time pressure. Clearly state:

  • The factual circumstances (name coincidence, writ, closing pressure)
  • That you consider the $480 charge unreasonable given the minimal skill, time, and complexity involved
  • That you intend to pursue assessment and/or Small Claims Court if not reimbursed within 21 days

Sometimes a firm will quietly settle a $480 complaint rather than have it escalate — especially in writing where it creates a record. It costs you nothing but a stamp, and it demonstrates you're serious before you file.

The Media Angle Complements This Well

CBC Marketplace or even a personal finance columnist (Rob Carrick at the Globe comes to mind) would find "Ontario homebuyer forced to pay law firm $480 to prove they're not someone else's debtor" genuinely compelling. The combination of a pending Small Claims filing and visible media interest changes the calculus for the firm considerably — reputational risk suddenly enters the equation alongside the litigation nuisance.

Bottom Line

You're right — for $102 you can impose hundreds of dollars in real cost on the firm, with minimal personal risk, while keeping genuine settlement pressure alive through the mandatory conference process. It's not irrational at all. It's just litigation economics used deliberately at small scale.
